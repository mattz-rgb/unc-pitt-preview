The setup

North Carolina heads to Pittsburgh as a four point underdog, and the number is about right. ESPN's odds show Pitt at -4 after an opener of -4.5, Covers has most books between -3 and -3.5, the Tar Heels sit somewhere between +130 and +150 on the moneyline, and the total is 46.5. UNC is 2-2 and 0-1 in the ACC after two losses that were winnable for most of three quarters each. At Clemson on September 19, the Tar Heels led 17-6 at the half and 20-15 in the fourth quarter before being outscored 22-3 after the break in a 28-20 loss. Against No. 3 Notre Dame last Saturday, they trailed 21-14 at the half, pulled within 21-20 when Jaden Jefferson returned the second half kickoff 98 yards, and then watched the Irish score 16 straight points in a 37-26 loss. Pitt is 5-0 for the second time in three years, ranked No. 25 in the AP poll and No. 24 in the coaches poll for the first time this season, and coming off a 35-33 win at Virginia Tech that looked a lot better in the final score than it did on the stat sheet.

The thing that changed this game happened on the fourth play of the second half in Blacksburg. Pitt starting quarterback Mason Heintschel tore his ACL and is done for the season, which Pat Narduzzi confirmed over the weekend. Heintschel was 98 of 148 (66.2 percent) for 1,304 yards, 14 touchdowns and one interception, including a 453 yard, seven touchdown opener against Miami (Ohio) that tied the Pitt single game record and set an ACC mark. In his place steps fifth year senior Holden Geriner, 23 years old, on his third school in five years after stops at Auburn and Texas State, who has thrown just 26 passes this season (19 completions, 219 yards, two touchdowns, no interceptions). He will make the first real start of his college career against a Bill Belichick defense, and that is the single biggest variable on the board. The point spread appears to have the news priced in, which is why it is only four points against an unbeaten, ranked home team.

The environment will not be friendly. Acrisure Stadium (51,416, natural grass) is sold out, Pitt is celebrating the 50th anniversary of its 1976 national championship team, and the Panthers are 4-0 at home, outscoring opponents 157-34. It is UNC's fifth consecutive noon kickoff. Belichick has history in this building, back when it was Heinz Field: two of his AFC Championship Game wins with the Patriots, in 2001 and 2004, came here. The forecast as of Tuesday calls for a high near 74, clear skies and roughly a 10 percent chance of rain. That matters a little, because UNC's last two games were played through a lightning delay of about two and a half hours at Clemson and a steady rain against Notre Dame, and the weather shaped how both were called. Saturday looks like a dry, fast track, so this one should be decided by football rather than conditions. Forecasts four days out move, so treat that as provisional.

The stakes are simple. A win moves UNC to 3-2 and 1-1 in the ACC and gets the program its first conference win of the season. A loss puts the Tar Heels at 2-3 and 0-2 with Duke (one of the ACC's three remaining unbeaten teams, October 17 in Durham), Syracuse and No. 4 Miami coming next. UNC has eight games left including this one and needs four wins in those eight to reach bowl eligibility, so a road game against a team starting a first time quarterback is among the more winnable ones on the board. The series history is mixed: it is the 19th meeting since 1974, Pitt has won three of the past five, including 34-24 at Kenan Stadium on October 5, 2024, and the Panthers have taken two of the last three played in Pittsburgh.

The two teams

North Carolina: A Veteran Front, a Rebuilt Line and a Run First Identity

The 2025 team went 4-8, ranked 126th nationally in scoring offense and averaged just 105 rushing yards per game. More than 20 players left through the portal after the season, Freddie Kitchens was replaced at offensive coordinator by Bobby Petrino, and the staff put the bulk of its roster resources into an offensive line that returned only three players who had ever started a game. The result is an offense designed to run the football physically and use play action to create chunk plays off it, led by quarterback Billy Edwards Jr., and a defense built around a veteran defensive line and the defensive background of its head coach.

The four results tell the story of a team that is competitive but not yet consistent. UNC beat TCU 15-10 in Dublin, outgaining the Horned Frogs 322-281 despite running only 29 times for 90 yards, then rolled over FCS East Tennessee State 35-3 behind three Demon June touchdowns. At Clemson, the Tar Heels ran 39 times for 214 yards (Benjamin Hall had 130 on 18 carries, his first career 100 yard game), built that 17-6 halftime lead, and then managed only three second half points while Clemson's freshman quarterback Tait Reynolds, making his first start, went 20 of 29 for 215 yards. Against Notre Dame, UNC scored on its opening drive, answered a 21-7 deficit with a 15 play, 97 yard touchdown drive that ended with 21 seconds left in the half, and got the kickoff return touchdown. But the Irish held the ball for 37:04 to UNC's 22:56, outgained the Tar Heels 475-285 and ran for 241 yards to UNC's 77.

By my math, UNC is scoring 24.0 points per game and allowing 19.5. The pass defense, usually the steadier half of a Belichick unit, gave up four touchdown passes to Notre Dame's CJ Carr but held the Irish to 234 passing yards. The run defense is the real problem, with 427 rushing yards allowed over the last two weeks. The pattern in both losses is a second half fade. UNC has been outscored 38-15 after halftime across the Clemson and Notre Dame games, though the causes differ: at Clemson it was the offense that stalled, with Petrino drifting away from the run late when the Tar Heels had a chance to take control, and against Notre Dame it was the defense wearing down, giving up 166 rushing yards on 30 carries after the break.

There is also the context around the program that is hard to ignore. General manager Michael Lombardi resigned on September 3, and defensive coordinator Steve Belichick, who has been away from the team since early August, announced his resignation on September 17, effective October 27. Bill Belichick took over defensive play calling for the opener against TCU, according to reports, and I have not seen a report of a change since. None of it has visibly sunk the team on the field, but it does mean the head coach is calling the defense while managing the whole game, which makes the defense's week to week adjustments worth watching.

Pitt: A Defense First Program With a Quarterback Problem Nobody Planned For

Pat Narduzzi is 85-61 at Pitt (54-38 in ACC regular season games), and the Panthers have won eight of their last nine conference games. They went 8-5 and 6-2 in the ACC last year, then lost longtime defensive coordinator Randy Bates to retirement after the Military Bowl. Cory Sanders, who spent years coaching Pitt's safeties, took over the defense. Offensive coordinator Kade Bell runs a hurry up, no huddle spread, and Narduzzi brought in 16 transfers over the winter, including linebackers DeMarco Ward (Memphis) and Alex Sanford (Purdue), whom he singled out as impact players.

The five wins: 59-14 over Miami (Ohio), where Heintschel threw seven touchdowns and the defense allowed 28 rushing yards on 21 carries; 12-7 over UCF in a rain soaked game with a 65 minute lightning delay, where Pitt scored all 12 points in the first half and held UCF to nine first downs and 20 rushing yards; 27-13 over Syracuse; 59-0 over FCS Bucknell; and 35-33 at Virginia Tech. The offense averages 478.0 yards and 38.4 points per game, fourth in the ACC in both, and it throws the ball well: 316.0 passing yards per game (third in the ACC) and 17 touchdown passes (second), with 17 different players having caught a pass and 11 having caught a touchdown.

The record needs context, though. Two of the five wins came against Miami (Ohio) and an FCS opponent, one was a 12-7 grind, and the one real road test saw Pitt outgained 499-295 and out averaged 6.8 yards per play to 4.8 by a Virginia Tech team that turned the ball over four times and missed a 36 yard field goal as time expired. That does not make Pitt a paper tiger. It means the defense and the turnover margin (plus 5, second in the ACC) have been carrying a lot of weight, and that the margin for error is thinner than 5-0 suggests.

The defense is the identity. Pitt is third in the ACC in scoring defense (13.4 points per game) and second in rushing defense (55.6 yards per game), has held four of five opponents under 100 rushing yards, and has not allowed a third quarter point all season, outscoring opponents 42-0 in the period. Five different Panthers have intercepted a pass, and Pitt has picked off two passes in each of its first two ACC games. The soft spot, historically, is the secondary: Pitt allowed 235.3 passing yards per game in 2025, which ranked 99th nationally, and Virginia Tech's Ethan Grunkemeyer threw for a career high 434 yards against it last Friday.

The Broad Comparison

On paper, Pitt has the better roster in the trenches, at linebacker and in the kicking game, and its offense has more proven playmakers around the quarterback. North Carolina's advantages are at quarterback, tight end and kick returns, plus a defensive minded head coach with a career's worth of experience designing plans against inexperienced quarterbacks. This is not a case of a better team against a worse one so much as two teams whose strengths point at each other's weaknesses: UNC's run first offense against the ACC's second best run defense, and Pitt's run first offense against a UNC run defense that has been gashed twice in a row.

Position group breakdown

Quarterback

Billy Edwards Jr. has been functional and occasionally good, but not steady. Through four games he is 61 of 106 (57.5 percent) for 819 yards, six touchdowns and three interceptions, a 7.7 yards per attempt line that reflects an offense built on play action shots and short throws. He was efficient against East Tennessee State, with Jelani Thurman and Hall both scoring off play action, completed only 13 of 30 for 135 yards at Clemson, and then went 17 of 28 for 208 yards and a career tying three touchdowns against Notre Dame, including a 7 for 8, 77 yard march to close the first half. The turnovers are the issue. Both Notre Dame interceptions came in the first half, one on a wobbly throw as defensive end Boubacar Traore hit him and another on a long attempt down the left sideline, and the Clemson game ended with an interception as he tried to tie it. Edwards has more than 500 career Power 4 attempts and entered the season coming off a knee injury, and his arm strength has been described as adequate rather than special. Miles O'Neill, the Texas A&M transfer Petrino recruited in College Station, is the backup and got mop up time against ETSU.

Geriner is close to the opposite profile: a quarterback described as composed, with a good arm, and almost no game experience. He is 19 of 26 (73.1 percent) for 219 yards, two touchdowns and no interceptions in three appearances (Miami of Ohio, Bucknell and Virginia Tech), most of it in blowouts. At Virginia Tech he went 8 of 12 for 78 yards and a 29 yard touchdown to Cataurus Hicks that restored an 11 point lead early in the fourth quarter. Narduzzi has said his confidence comes from Geriner's demeanor and from Bell's track record of getting production from quarterbacks, which he saw with Heintschel last year. Freshman Corey Dailey, who was 3 for 5 with a touchdown against Bucknell, is now the backup. The question for Pitt is not whether Geriner can complete a throw. It is how he handles a defense that disguises coverage and pressure looks when the game is close and he is the one the other sideline is trying to confuse.

Edge: North Carolina, and it is the largest edge on the field. But it is worth less than usual, because Pitt's offense is built on its backs, its line and a deep receiver rotation, none of which left with Heintschel.

Running Back

North Carolina's backfield has been deeper than it has been productive, but it has found a lead back. Hall leads the team with 251 yards on 43 carries (5.8 per carry) after the 130 yard game at Clemson, and he has been a weapon in the passing game too, with a 15 yard touchdown catch at Clemson and a 26 yarder against East Tennessee State. June, the sophomore who led the 2025 team with 464 rushing yards at 5.5 per carry, has been the finisher, with a three touchdown game against ETSU and a 3 yard score on the opening drive at Clemson. Jaylen McGill rotates in and graded best among the backs in pass protection after the TCU game (77.9 from PFF). The caution is that the production has followed the game script: 214 yards when UNC was leading at Clemson, 90 against TCU on 29 carries, and 77 on 23 carries when it was playing from behind against Notre Dame.

Pitt has the deeper and healthier room. True freshman Damon Ferguson Jr. leads the team with 270 rushing yards (5.3 per carry) and four touchdowns through five games, including the 15 yarder at Virginia Tech that gave the Panthers their first lead. La'Vell Wright has 145 yards and two touchdowns, and Ja'Kyrian Turner, who missed the UCF game with an undisclosed injury, returned at Virginia Tech for 65 rushing yards, a touchdown and six catches, 100 yards from scrimmage in all. Narduzzi noted this week that all three backs are healthy again, and with a new quarterback the Panthers are averaging 162.0 rushing yards per game and will lean harder on it. Edge: Pitt, narrowly, on depth and the line in front of it. This is where the game gets interesting, because it pits UNC's identity against Pitt's: both teams want to run, and only one of them is facing a defense that has allowed 55.6 yards per game on the ground.

Receivers and Tight Ends

Jordan Shipp leads UNC's receivers with 20 catches for 273 yards, including a career high seven for 109 against Notre Dame and a 44 yard catch and run that was the longest play of UNC's season at that point. The tight ends are the most dependable part of the group. Thurman has three touchdowns on the year (one against ETSU and two against Notre Dame) after a four catch, 94 yard game in the opener against TCU, and Shamar Easter caught the first touchdown of his career, a 6 yarder, on the opening drive against Notre Dame. Mason Humphrey, the Lehigh transfer who averaged 18.6 yards per catch last season, and Trech Kekahuna were the projected starters alongside Shipp and give Petrino vertical options. The passing game as a whole is the least consistent piece of the offense, at 135 yards against Clemson and 57.5 percent on the season.

Pitt's group is deep rather than dominant. Censere Lee leads the team with 18 receptions and four touchdown catches and needs 44 yards to reach 2,000 for his career. Hicks leads in receiving yards (315 on 15 catches, per ESPN) and in all purpose yards (394), and had two touchdown catches at Virginia Tech, the 22 yarder from Heintschel just before halftime and the 29 yarder from Geriner. Malik Knight and Tony Kinsler were battling for the third receiver spot in the opening depth chart, Oklahoma transfer Carson Kent won the starting tight end job, and Turner's six catches at Virginia Tech matched a career high out of the backfield. Seventeen Panthers have caught a pass, which tells you Bell spreads the football around and that Geriner will likely have easy answers rather than a single hero to find. Edge: North Carolina slightly, on the tight ends and the big play ability of Shipp, though this is close to a wash.

Offensive Line

The line was North Carolina's offseason project, and the first month has been a mixed review. Left tackle Jordan Hall (6 foot 8, 336 pounds) and right tackle Jacqawn McRoy (6 foot 8, 335) are enormous and still unproven in pass protection, right guard Aidan Banfield is the steadiest piece, center Christo Kelly is a Holy Cross transfer who is back healthy after missing most of last season, and left guard Andrew Threatt, a Charleston Southern transfer, can play inside or outside. Under line coach Will Friend the group got real push at Clemson (214 rushing yards) but not against Notre Dame (77), and after the TCU game PFF had Kelly highest overall (63.2) and Banfield highest in run blocking (60.7). The concern going in was pass protection in a scheme that leaves tackles on an island, and Edwards was hit as he threw on the first Notre Dame interception.

Pitt's line is more experienced and more settled. Left tackle Netinho Olivieri, left guard Kendall Stanley, center Ryan Carretta, right guard BJ Williams and right tackle Ryan Baer opened the year as the starters, with Jiavani Cooley, Keylen Davis, Keith Gouveia, Torian Chester and Mason Lindsay Jr. as the second unit. The group has helped produce 162.0 rushing yards and 478.0 total yards per game, though the offense managed only 104 rushing yards and 295 total at Virginia Tech. I do not have reliable sacks allowed numbers for either team, and Pitt's depth chart may have shifted since Week 1, so treat those names as the opening week lineup. Edge: Pitt, clearly. UNC's best hope up front is that its size and a game plan heavy on quick throws and play action keep Pitt's front from dictating the terms.

Defensive Line

North Carolina's defensive line was supposed to be the strength of the roster, and it remains the most experienced group, but the injury news makes it the group to track this week. Redshirt sophomore Leroy Jackson played a season high 60 snaps against Notre Dame. Isaiah Johnson, the 320 pound redshirt senior who is tied for third on the team in PFF run defense grade (75.7, along with Xavier Lewis), left the Notre Dame game hurt. Belichick said Monday that Johnson is 'coming along' but would not project a return for Saturday, so treat his status as unconfirmed. In his absence, true freshmen Nicco Maggio (the nose tackle behind Johnson) and Trey Giddens played 23 and 21 snaps, the most either has seen against an FBS opponent, and Notre Dame ran for 166 yards on 30 carries in the second half. Lewis, a former walk on, has been the surprise of the group, and ESPN's team leaders list him with six sacks. Jaylen Harvey was disruptive against East Tennessee State, and Melkart Abou-Jaoude, a projected starter, appeared close to returning before the Clemson game but was not available; I could not confirm his status for Saturday.

Pitt's front is the engine of the second ranked rushing defense in the ACC. Isaiah Neal has been the standout, with 14 tackles, three tackles for loss, a team high 1.5 sacks, four quarterback hurries and a forced fumble that he also recovered, and Narduzzi raves about his instincts. Zach Crothers, Jimmy Scott and Blaine Spires have rotated on the edges. This is a physical group rather than an explosive one (a 2025 scouting report noted that Pitt's ends win with power rather than speed), and it is not a sack heavy unit, since a good share of the pressure has come from the linebackers. What it does is stop the run: 28 yards on 21 carries against Miami (Ohio), 20 yards against UCF and 65 at Virginia Tech.

Edge: Pitt, and it is likely the most lopsided matchup on the field if Johnson cannot play. Pitt's offensive line against UNC's interior rotation is where I would worry first.

Linebackers

Derek McDonald, the Syracuse transfer who had 173 tackles in his first four seasons before missing most of 2025 with an injury, has been the center of UNC's defense and leads the team with 36 tackles, per ESPN. Peyton Seelmann, who came over from FCS Richmond, plays with anticipation and effort, though one scouting note observed that his tape shows him absorbing contact more often than delivering it. Both starters are first year Tar Heels, former NFL linebacker Jamie Collins coaches the inside group, and they are still learning to play together. Notre Dame's 5.6 yards per carry on runs up the middle, per PFF, is partly a defensive line problem, but linebackers playing behind a thinner interior have to be decisive, and tackling in space will matter against a Pitt offense that spreads the field and then runs.

Pitt lost two impact linebackers from last year's group and rebuilt through the portal. Braylan Lovelace, the senior from nearby Leechburg, anchors the unit, with Jeremiah Marcelin and Sanford starting alongside him in the opener and Ward a major piece of the rotation. Ward brings 63 tackles and two pick sixes from his 2025 season at Memphis, and Cameron Lindsey (32 tackles and 5.0 tackles for loss last season) intercepted Grunkemeyer at Virginia Tech. This group generates much of the pressure: Ward and Marcelin combined to sack UCF's quarterback on fourth down to end that game, and Sanford added a fourth quarter sack. They fly to the football, they are productive against the run, and Narduzzi's scheme is designed to let them do both. Edge: Pitt.

Secondary

North Carolina's projected starting secondary entering the year was Jaiden Patterson and Ade Willie at corner, Kaleb Cost at nickel, Greg Smith at strong safety and Coleman Bryson at free safety, with Marcus Allen in the rotation. I have not confirmed this week's depth chart, but those are the names that have featured. The unit has done a decent job limiting yardage (Notre Dame passed for 234 yards, a modest total given the four touchdowns), but it has had communication breakdowns, including a blown coverage that gave TCU its first touchdown in the opener, and the Irish exploited them early. After Edwards's first interception, Carr needed one play to hit Jaden Greathouse for a 12 yard touchdown, and Jordan Faison caught eight passes for 132 yards and two scores. Patterson said afterward that the second half problems came down to a few miscommunications and not executing the small things, and that when the defense played complementary football it was effective. Smith had the interception at Clemson. The test this week is different from last week's: Pitt wants to run, which puts the safeties in the tackling business and makes eye discipline against play action the thing to watch.

Pitt's secondary remains the question mark, though it comes with more bodies and a defensive coordinator who came up coaching defensive backs; Narduzzi pointed out this summer that Bates was a linebackers guy and Sanders is a secondary coach. Cruce Brookins, a Steel Valley product, leads the team with 26 tackles per ESPN and had two interceptions as a sophomore last year, and Narduzzi has called him a ballhawk. I do not have a reliable full depth chart for Pitt's back end. The results are mixed: UCF's Alonza Barnett completed 20 of 31 passes for just 186 yards, but Grunkemeyer put up 434, and Virginia Tech averaged 6.8 yards per play. At the same time, the group takes the ball away. Edge: even, with the caveat that this is Pitt's weakest defensive unit going against UNC's least consistent offensive unit.

Where North Carolina should target

The case for UNC rests on a handful of specific advantages, and none of them requires the Tar Heels to beat Pitt at what Pitt does best.

The first is the back end of Pitt's defense. The Panthers have been excellent against the run and in the turnover column, but the secondary is where opponents have found room: Grunkemeyer threw for a career high 434 yards, and Pitt ranked 99th nationally in pass defense a year ago. UNC's offense is at its best when the run game and play action work together, as at ETSU when Edwards faked to Hall and Thurman leaked out for a 25 yard touchdown, and against Notre Dame when Shipp's 44 yard catch and run powered the opening drive and Easter and Thurman scored. Shipp and Humphrey give Petrino vertical options, and the tight end seam is the most reliable route to the end zone. Pitt's linebackers are built to attack the run, so if they stay aggressive the play action should be there.

The second is Geriner. Belichick's career is the case study in disrupting inexperienced quarterbacks, and this is the situation where his preparation matters most. Pitt wants to run (162.0 yards per game, Ferguson at 5.3 per carry), so the first goal is to make first and second down uncomfortable: load the box, disguise the safeties, and make Geriner complete throws against coverage that rotates after the snap. He has attempted only 26 passes this season, 12 of them at Virginia Tech, and what he has not seen is a defense that shows one look and plays another. UNC has the pass rush production to make that work in Lewis, Harvey and Jackson. Getting Pitt into third and seven or longer, where Narduzzi's offense has to put the game on a first time starter, is the best defensive outcome available.

Third, start fast and sustain drives. UNC scored on its opening drive against Notre Dame (74 yards in seven plays), put together a 15 play, 97 yard touchdown drive before halftime, and built early leads at Clemson. Pitt has not allowed a third quarter point, which means the Tar Heels need to bank points before the break rather than count on a fast restart. A lead entering the third quarter forces Pitt into the game it least wants, a first time starter chasing points. Notre Dame won the time of possession 37:04 to 22:56; reversing that would be one of the biggest wins of the day, because every long UNC drive is a possession Ferguson, Wright and Turner do not get.

Fourth, the return game and hidden yardage. Pitt's coverage units are an unknown to me, but field position matters in a game with a 46.5 total, and Jefferson is the closest thing UNC has to a proven game breaker on special teams.

Fifth, treat the quick game as the run game. Pitt allows 55.6 rushing yards per game, so the answer is not necessarily more carries between the tackles. It is screens and short throws to Hall, who already has two receiving touchdowns this season, and run pass concepts that keep Pitt's linebackers from tracing the ball. That is my read of the matchup rather than anything Petrino has said.

Where North Carolina should be concerned

The first concern is the one at the center of the game: UNC's run defense against a Pitt team that now has every reason to run. Notre Dame gained 241 rushing yards at 5.4 per carry, Clemson gained roughly 186, and that adds up to 427 rushing yards allowed in two weeks. Notre Dame averaged 5.6 yards per carry up the middle and 5.3 between the tackles, and gained 166 yards on 30 carries in the second half after Johnson left. Narduzzi said this week that Pitt will lean on the run game with a new quarterback, and his backfield is healthy. If Johnson is out again, true freshmen are the interior of the line against a veteran Pitt offensive line. Notre Dame's 17 play, 75 yard drive to open the second half is the template, and Pitt's quarterback does not need to be good for that kind of drive to work.

The second concern is the other side of that matchup. UNC's offense is built to run the football, and Pitt allows 55.6 yards per game on the ground, has held four of five opponents under 100, and has allowed 34 points in four home games. A run first team that gets stuffed on early downs becomes a pass first team on long yardage, which brings Edwards's turnover risk into play against a defense with five different interceptors, two picks in each ACC game and a plus 5 turnover margin. Edwards has thrown three interceptions on 106 attempts, but two of them came in one half against Notre Dame, and Pitt turned four Virginia Tech turnovers into 14 points. In a game with a four point spread, a pair of giveaways is usually decisive.

Third, the second half. UNC has been outscored 38-15 after halftime in its two losses, and Pitt has outscored opponents 42-0 in the third quarter. The Tar Heels' defense played a season high number of snaps against Notre Dame in part because the offense could not keep the ball, and the Irish outgained UNC by 134 yards in the second half after the kickoff return touchdown. Wearing down late is a real risk against a team that wants to run 70 plays and has the line to do it.

Fourth, kicking. Burrell missed all three of his attempts from 40 to 49 yards entering the Notre Dame game, and UNC lost a point on a botched snap and another on a blocked extra point last week. Pitt's Chadha is perfect on the year. In a game with a four point spread and a 46.5 total, a missed field goal or extra point is a large share of the margin.

Fifth, Pitt's offense is still dangerous. Seventeen different Panthers have caught passes, Lee and Hicks are proven, Pitt averages 38.4 points per game, and the structure of Bell's offense is intact even with Geriner. If UNC's secondary suffers the same coverage breakdowns that allowed four touchdown passes to Carr, Pitt will find them. And there is the emotional factor: the Panthers are playing at home, in front of a sold out crowd, for a teammate who is out for the year, with a ranking in hand for the first time. That counts for something, and it is the kind of thing a road team needs to survive in the first quarter.

How the game breaks down

Here is how I think the afternoon unfolds. It is one plausible path rather than a drive chart, and it is specific on purpose so it can be judged afterward.

The first quarter belongs to the scripts. Pitt, with a new quarterback, will run early and often, using Ferguson and Wright between the tackles and short, high percentage throws to give Geriner a rhythm of completions on first and second down. UNC has scored early in its last two games, with an opening drive touchdown against Notre Dame and a touchdown barely four minutes into the Clemson game, and I expect play action to a tight end to produce an early score. UNC 7, Pitt 0 after one.

Pitt's offense is built to answer, and Chadha's range means any drive past midfield is worth points. I expect a Pitt field goal and then a touchdown in the second quarter, with UNC adding a touchdown of its own for a 14-10 Tar Heel lead at the half. The sequences that matter most in this stretch are Pitt's first third and long and Edwards's first third and medium. Whoever wins those sets the tone for the half.

The third quarter is where Pitt has been unbeatable all season, and I expect the trend to hold in some form. Think of a long Pitt drive built on the run game, similar to Notre Dame's 17 play drive to start the second half, ending in a Wright or Ferguson touchdown to put the Panthers up 17-14. This is the stretch where UNC either lives or dies on its run defense, and where the absence of Johnson would show.

The fourth quarter is the swing. UNC's offense, which went quiet in the second half at Clemson, needs an answer, and I think it gets one: a field goal to tie it at 17, a Pitt touchdown to go up 24-17, and then the drive of the game, Edwards working play action against a Pitt defense that has been run focused all afternoon, to tie it at 24. The defense then has to get one stop, which is where the second half fade needs to end, and the Tar Heels take the lead on a late field goal for 27-24.
